1. Foundations and developmental research

Developmental psychology studies patterns of growth and change across the entire lifespan. Development includes physical, cognitive, and social-emotional changes.

Nature and nurture

Concept Meaning Scenario clue
Nature Inherited and biological influences. Genetic similarities or maturation.
Nurture Environmental influences and experience. Parenting, education, social expectations, and culture.
Interaction Biological and environmental influences work together. A developing ability depends on both maturation and experience.

Similarities between identical twins raised apart can provide evidence consistent with genetic influence. They do not prove that environment has no effect.

Similarities among genetically unrelated children raised in the same family can help researchers examine shared environmental influences. No single comparison perfectly isolates every genetic or environmental factor.

Research designs

Design What researchers do Main limitation
Cross-sectional Compare different age groups at one point in time. Age differences may be mixed with cohort differences.
Longitudinal Study the same participants repeatedly over time. Time, cost, participant loss, and repeated-testing effects.
Sequential Follow more than one age cohort over time. More complex and resource intensive.

A cohort is a group sharing a historical period or experience, such as people born during a similar span of years.

Cross-sectional = different people, same time.
Longitudinal = same people, different times.
Cohort = shared historical context.

If 20-year-olds and 60-year-olds perform differently today, the difference may reflect age, schooling, technology exposure, or other historical experiences.

Check yourself: A researcher reassesses the same children every six months. Which design?

Longitudinal research.

2. Genetics and prenatal development

Genes, DNA, and chromosomes

  • DNA: the molecule carrying genetic information.
  • Genes: functional segments of DNA involved in biological processes and inheritance.
  • Chromosomes: structures containing DNA and many genes.
  • Typical human zygote: 46 chromosomes arranged in 23 pairs.
  • Zygote: the single cell formed by fertilization.

The bank uses the typical introductory association XX → female development and XY → male development. Biological sex development is more complex than chromosomes alone, and variations occur.

The Human Genome Project produced a reference sequence and maps of the human genome. It did not establish a simple one-gene explanation for every human trait.

The bank’s claim that the project mapped “every human gene” is simplified. The original project produced an essentially complete reference for its time, with difficult regions resolved by later research.

Prenatal stages

Stage Approximate timing after conception Key features
Germinal First two weeks. Fertilization, cell division, and implantation.
Embryonic Approximately weeks 2–8. Formation of major body structures and organs.
Fetal After the embryonic period through birth. Growth, increasing differentiation, and maturation of body systems.
Germinal → embryonic → fetal.
Zygote → embryo → fetus.

Timing terms

  • Critical period: a relatively narrow interval when particular input is especially necessary for typical development.
  • Sensitive period: a period of heightened responsiveness to experience; change may still be possible outside it.
  • Viability: the possibility of survival outside the uterus with available care.
  • Preterm birth: birth before 37 completed weeks of pregnancy, using obstetric gestational dating.

Sensitive and critical periods can occur before or after birth.

Do not mix dating systems. Time since conception differs from obstetric gestational age, which is usually counted from the last menstrual period. The bank uses “22 weeks after conception” as a simplified viability answer. Survival is not guaranteed at one fixed week and depends on multiple clinical factors.

3. Prenatal risks and newborn abilities

Inherited and chromosomal conditions

Condition Chapter association
Phenylketonuria: PKU Difficulty processing phenylalanine; untreated buildup can harm development.
Tay-Sachs disease An inherited condition involving harmful accumulation of a fatty substance in nerve cells.
Down syndrome Usually associated with an extra copy of chromosome 21.
Sickle-cell disease An inherited hemoglobin condition affecting red blood cells.

The bank’s severe PKU outcome refers to untreated disease. Early identification and treatment can prevent major developmental harm. Tay-Sachs is more specific than a general inability to digest all fat, and different forms have different courses.

Teratogens

A teratogen is an exposure that can disrupt prenatal development. Effects depend on factors such as timing, dose, and individual susceptibility.

The bank’s medication example illustrates a potential teratogenic exposure. It is a classification question, not a reason for a pregnant person to start or stop prescribed medication independently.

Fetal alcohol syndrome is associated with prenatal alcohol exposure and can involve growth differences, characteristic facial features, and neurodevelopmental difficulties. It is part of the broader range of fetal alcohol spectrum disorders.

Newborn reflexes

A newborn is a neonate.

Reflex Recognition clue
Rooting Turning the head toward a touch on the cheek.
Sucking Sucking in response to appropriate stimulation around the mouth.
Startle / Moro A characteristic arm response to sudden stimulation or loss of support.
Babinski Toes fan outward when the sole is stroked in the characteristic way.
Gag A protective response to stimulation near the back of the mouth or throat.

Habituation

Habituation is a decrease in responding after repeated presentation of the same stimulus. It can help researchers study infant attention and learning.

Rooting = turn toward cheek touch.
Babinski = toes fan.
Habituation = response decreases with repetition.

4. Attachment

Attachment is an enduring emotional bond with a caregiver. It is a central part of early social development.

Researcher or method Main connection
Konrad Lorenz Imprinting in certain species during an early sensitive or critical period.
Harry Harlow Contact comfort was important in infant monkeys’ attachment behavior.
Mary Ainsworth The Strange Situation used separation and reunion to examine attachment patterns.

Imprinting is not a complete explanation of human attachment. Harlow’s findings also do not mean infants do not need food. The key lesson is that attachment cannot be explained by feeding alone.

Attachment patterns

Pattern Typical Strange Situation clues
Secure Uses caregiver as a secure base, seeks comfort, and is soothed by reunion.
Avoidant Shows relatively little overt seeking of contact and may avoid the caregiver at reunion.
Ambivalent / resistant Strong distress, difficulty being soothed, and a mixture of seeking and resisting contact.
Disorganized Contradictory, disoriented, or apprehensive behavior without a consistent strategy.
Secure = explore, seek comfort, settle.
Avoidant = limited overt contact seeking.
Ambivalent = seek and resist.

Sensitive, responsive caregiving supports attachment. Responding to both positive and negative signals matters. No single behavior guarantees a particular attachment classification.

Attachment classifications describe relationship patterns, not fixed character flaws or a child’s inevitable future. Associations with later adjustment are probabilistic and depend on context.

5. Play, parenting, temperament, and resilience

Play and social experience

Parallel play occurs when children play near one another with limited coordination of their activities. It is different from organized cooperative play.

Social interaction provides opportunities to interpret others’ behavior, practice communication, and respond appropriately. High-quality early care can support development, particularly when children face limited access to resources.

The bank associates fathers with more physical or rough-and-tumble play on average. Treat this as a group-level generalization in the text, not a rule for every father, mother, or family.

Parenting styles

Style Typical pattern Scenario clue
Authoritarian High demands, low responsiveness, emphasis on obedience. “Follow the rule without questioning.”
Authoritative Clear expectations combined with warmth, explanation, and responsiveness. Firm limits plus reasons and discussion.
Permissive High responsiveness with relatively few demands or limits. Warm and tolerant but few rules.
Uninvolved Low responsiveness and low involvement or demands. Little engagement or guidance.
Authoritarian = obedience.
Authoritative = expectations + explanation.
Permissive = few limits.
Uninvolved = little engagement.

Temperament and resilience

  • Temperament: early-emerging differences in emotional reactivity and self-regulation.
  • Resilience: adapting relatively well despite significant adversity.

Development is bidirectional: children influence caregivers, and caregivers influence children. A child’s temperament can help shape the responses adults provide.

Resilience is supported by relationships and resources. It does not mean a child is unaffected by hardship or must cope alone.

6. Erikson’s eight psychosocial stages

Erikson described psychosocial development across the lifespan. Each stage highlights a central challenge involving the person and the social world.

Approximate period Conflict Main question or positive direction
Infancy Trust vs. mistrust Can I depend on others to meet my needs?
Toddlerhood Autonomy vs. shame and doubt Can I do things for myself?
Preschool years Initiative vs. guilt Can I start activities and pursue goals?
School-age years Industry vs. inferiority Can I develop skills and feel competent?
Adolescence Identity vs. role confusion Who am I, and what matters to me?
Young adulthood Intimacy vs. isolation Can I form close, committed relationships?
Middle adulthood Generativity vs. stagnation How can I contribute to future generations?
Later adulthood Integrity vs. despair Can I accept and find meaning in my life?
Infant → trust.
Toddler → autonomy.
Preschool → initiative.
School age → industry.
Adolescent → identity.
Young adult → intimacy.
Middle adult → generativity.
Older adult → integrity.

Scenario clues

  • Unpredictable care → trust/mistrust.
  • Trying to dress independently → autonomy/shame and doubt.
  • Wanting to help or initiate a project → initiative/guilt.
  • Developing school skills and competence → industry/inferiority.
  • Exploring values and future roles → identity/role confusion.
  • Forming close relationships → intimacy/isolation.
  • Mentoring and contributing → generativity/stagnation.
  • Evaluating and accepting one’s life → integrity/despair.

Generativity can involve mentoring, teaching, creativity, community work, or caregiving. It does not require marriage or parenthood.

The theory’s lifespan scope is a strength. Criticisms include cultural and gender assumptions and difficulty testing broad constructs. Ages and challenges are not rigid deadlines, and one difficult interaction does not determine a child’s future.

7. Piaget’s stages of cognitive development

Piaget proposed qualitative changes in thinking, not simply the accumulation of more facts. His account emphasizes interaction between maturation and experience.

Stage Approximate age Key features
Sensorimotor Birth to about 2 years. Learning through action and sensation; development of object permanence.
Preoperational About 2–7 years. Symbolic thought and language; difficulty with some perspective-taking and conservation tasks.
Concrete operational About 7–11 years. Logical operations involving concrete situations; conservation and reversibility.
Formal operational About 12 years onward. Abstract, hypothetical, and systematic reasoning.
Sensorimotor → preoperational → concrete operational → formal operational.

Concepts to distinguish

Concept Meaning Example
Object permanence Objects continue to exist when out of sight. Searching for a hidden toy.
Egocentrism Difficulty appreciating a perspective different from one’s own. Assuming another person sees exactly what you see.
Conservation Quantity remains the same despite a change in appearance. The same juice remains the same amount in a taller glass.
Reversibility An operation can be mentally reversed. Recognizing that pouring the juice back restores its original appearance.

A child who says a taller glass contains more juice after watching the same liquid being poured has difficulty with conservation, not necessarily object permanence.

A child can use words and symbols before mastering logical operations. Concrete operational reasoning is logical but tied more closely to concrete situations; formal operations emphasize hypothetical possibilities.

These ages are approximate descriptions within the theory. Children’s performance depends on the task, experience, instruction, and context. Modern research does not treat stage boundaries as exact limits on what every child can learn.

8. Information processing and Vygotsky

Information-processing approach

This approach studies changes in attention, memory, processing efficiency, strategies, and self-monitoring. As some processes become more automatic, resources can become available for other parts of a task.

Metacognition means knowledge and regulation of one’s own thinking. Recognizing that a memory strategy failed and selecting a better one illustrates metacognition.

Vygotsky’s sociocultural approach

Vygotsky emphasized the role of culture, language, and social interaction in cognitive development.

Concept Meaning Example
Zone of proximal development: ZPD The range between independent performance and what is possible with appropriate assistance. A child solves a problem with useful prompts but cannot yet solve it alone.
Scaffolding Adjustable support that helps a learner perform a task and is reduced as competence increases. A teacher models one step, then gradually removes prompts.
Piaget: changes in the organization of thinking.
Information processing: changes in strategies and efficiency.
Vygotsky: learning through culture and supported interaction.
Check yourself: A child succeeds with a parent’s prompts but not alone. Which concept fits?

The task is within the child’s zone of proximal development.

9. Adolescence and moral reasoning

Puberty and identity

Puberty is the biological process of sexual maturation. Adolescence is a broader developmental transition involving physical, cognitive, and social changes.

Erikson associates adolescence with identity vs. role confusion. Peers can become especially important sources of feedback, but families and other relationships still matter.

Adolescent egocentrism involves heightened self-focus and difficulty separating one’s own concerns from others’ perspectives. An imaginary audience involves feeling watched or judged; a personal fable involves beliefs about one’s uniqueness or special invulnerability.

Kohlberg’s moral reasoning

Level Reasoning emphasizes Example reason
Preconventional Personal consequences, punishment, reward, or self-interest. “I could be punished or hurt.”
Conventional Approval, rules, duties, and maintaining social order. “It is my duty to obey the rule.”
Postconventional Rights, social contracts, and ethical principles. “A principle of human rights matters even if a law conflicts with it.”
Judge the reason, not just the action. Two people can make the same choice for different moral reasons.

Criticisms include limits to cultural generality and assumptions about which moral concerns should define development. The theory concerns moral reasoning; reasoning and actual behavior are not identical.

Recognizing concerning changes

The bank includes a scenario involving withdrawal and appetite loss. Such changes can indicate distress and merit attention. A few behaviors in a short vignette are not enough to rank an actual person’s suicide risk or make a diagnosis.

10. Adulthood and family life

Emerging adulthood

Emerging adulthood refers to a period often described as extending from the late teens through the twenties, involving exploration of education, work, relationships, and identity.

The bank emphasizes the late teens through the mid-twenties. This life pattern is influenced by culture and opportunity; it is not a universal stage experienced in the same way by everyone.

Adult psychosocial challenges

  • Young adulthood: intimacy vs. isolation.
  • Middle adulthood: generativity vs. stagnation.
  • Later adulthood: integrity vs. despair.

Menopause

Menopause is the end of menstrual cycles associated with reproductive aging, conventionally identified after 12 months without menstruation when another cause does not explain it. It is different from menarche, the first menstrual period.

Family structures and development

Families vary in marital status, household composition, caregiving arrangements, employment, and social support. Many adults combine paid work with caregiving and household responsibilities.

Family income, conflict, stability, supportive relationships, and access to resources matter for children’s outcomes. A household label alone does not determine competence or adjustment.

Several bank items make broad predictions about divorce, single-parent families, and gender roles. Treat dated trend claims as claims to verify against assigned materials, not rules for an individual family. Increased economic risk does not mean an inevitable poor outcome.

11. Aging

Biological theories

Theory Chapter emphasis Recognition clue
Genetic programming Biological timing and limits related to cellular processes. A built-in limit on cell division.
Wear-and-tear Accumulating damage, cellular errors, and byproducts over time. Damage and errors accumulate.

These are broad theoretical emphases, not complete or mutually exclusive explanations of every aspect of aging.

Cognitive changes

Remembering specific personal events involves episodic memory. General factual knowledge involves semantic memory. These can show different patterns of change.

Alzheimer’s disease is a progressive brain disease affecting memory and other cognitive abilities. It is not an inevitable or normal part of aging.

Social theories

Theory Main idea Scenario clue
Disengagement theory A historical account emphasizing withdrawal from roles and social involvement. Reduced social engagement.
Activity theory Maintaining meaningful activity and roles supports well-being. Volunteering, hobbies, work, and social participation.
Programming = biological limits.
Wear-and-tear = accumulated damage.
Disengagement = withdrawal.
Activity = meaningful participation.

Withdrawal in a real person can have many explanations. A historical theory is not a diagnosis or proof that withdrawal is inevitable or desirable.

12. Life review and end-of-life theories

Life review

Life review involves examining and evaluating one’s experiences, relationships, accomplishments, and regrets. It connects with Erikson’s integrity-versus-despair theme.

Kübler-Ross’s proposed reactions

Reaction Typical textbook clue
Denial “This cannot be happening.”
Anger “Why is this happening to me?”
Bargaining “If I could only live until this important event…”
Depression Sadness in response to loss or impending death.
Acceptance Acknowledging the reality of what is happening.
The bank’s “let me live until…” example = bargaining.

These proposed reactions are not a mandatory, universal sequence. People may experience some, none, or several in changing order. Do not use the model to tell someone how they must grieve.
